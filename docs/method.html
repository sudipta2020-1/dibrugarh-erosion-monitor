This system maps river bank erosion, land change and soil erosion risk for Dibrugarh and Majuli districts. It updates itself every week from free satellite data. This page describes how each step is calculated. The boxes marked "Latest runs" show the values computed for each district in the most recent run. District results are on the two tabs above.

Overview of the processing chain

Each weekly run follows the same chain for both districts. A GitHub Actions job runs the Python pipeline. It writes the results as web layers, and GitHub Pages publishes the dashboards that this site embeds.

Steps and calculations

1

Study area and monitoring windows

The district boundary is fixed once. Dibrugarh uses the geoBoundaries ADM2 polygon. Majuli became a district in 2016 and is missing from older boundary sets, so its polygon is built from the OpenStreetMap administrative relation (admin_level 5). All layers are placed on one grid in UTM zone 46N (EPSG:32646) at 20 m resolution.

Each run compares the latest 120 days with the same calendar dates in the baseline year 2020. Comparing the same months keeps differences in river level and crop stage small, so they are not mistaken for change.

2

Satellite data and composites

Images are read from the Microsoft Planetary Computer catalogue, which is free and needs no account. Three datasets are used: Sentinel-2 L2A surface reflectance (bands B02, B03, B04, B08, B11, B12), Sentinel-1 radiometrically terrain-corrected radar (VV, VH), and the Copernicus 30 m DEM. Clouds, shadows and cirrus are removed with the Sentinel-2 scene classification: only classes 4 (vegetation), 5 (bare soil), 6 (water) and 7 (unclassified) are kept. At most the 15 clearest scenes of each tile are used. A per-pixel median of the clear observations gives one composite for each window. Radar sees through clouds and is used where the optical view is missing.

3

Spectral indices

Three indices are computed from the composites (B = Sentinel-2 band):

NDVI = (B08 − B04) / (B08 + B04) vegetation greenness MNDWI = (B03 − B11) / (B03 + B11) open water (Xu, 2006) BSI = [(B11 + B04) − (B08 + B02)] / [(B11 + B04) + (B08 + B02)] bare soil
4

Water mapping with a frequency rule

A single composite can be misled by one flooded or hazy date. The system therefore classifies every clear date separately and counts how often each pixel was water. The threshold is chosen automatically by the edge-based Otsu method (Donchyts et al., 2016): pixels near the land–water edge are selected, and the threshold that best separates their MNDWI values into two groups is used. For radar, the same is done on VV backscatter (in dB).

water frequency f = k / n k = number of clear dates on which the pixel was water, n = number of clear dates pixel is water if f ≥ 0.5 and n ≥ 2
5

Change detection

The baseline and current water maps and indices are compared pixel by pixel. Land-to-water change is then split by the JRC Global Surface Water record (Landsat, 1984–2021, Pekel et al., 2016). Loss of land that had never been water is bank erosion of stable land. Loss inside the historical river belt is char or sandbar loss. A loss patch that does not touch a water body of at least 50 ha is a pond or flooded field, not a river bank.

ClassRule
1 Bank erosion (stable land)land in baseline, water now, never water in 1984–2021, touches the river
2 Accretionwater in baseline, land now
3 Vegetation lossland in both windows and ΔNDVI = NDVIcurrent − NDVIbaseline < −0.15
4 New bare soilland now, BSI > 0.05 now and ≤ 0.05 in baseline
5 Char or sandbar lossland to water inside the JRC river belt, touches the river
6 New inland waterland to water not touching a water body ≥ 50 ha

Patches smaller than 10 pixels (0.4 ha) are dropped as noise.

6

Bank retreat

For every pixel that turned into water, the distance to the nearest baseline water pixel is how far the bank moved back. It is computed with a Euclidean distance transform. The rate divides the largest distance in a patch by the years between the two windows.

retreat rate (m/yr) = max distance to baseline water (m) / years between windows
7

Soil loss by RUSLE

Sheet and rill erosion on land is estimated with the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (Renard et al., 1997). Water pixels are excluded.

A = R × K × LS × C × P A = soil loss (t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) R = 79 + 0.363 Pmm rainfall erosivity; P = mean annual rainfall from IMD 0.25° gridded data, 2000–2025 (Singh et al., 1981; Pai et al., 2014). CHIRPS v2.0 is used as a cross-check. K from SoilGrids 250 m topsoil sand, silt, clay and organic carbon Williams (1995) EPIC equation LS = (λ / 22.13)m × S, S = 10.8 sin θ + 0.03 (tan θ < 0.09) or 16.8 sin θ − 0.50 θ from the Copernicus DEM smoothed over 100 m; λ = cell size (McCool et al., 1987) C = exp[−2 × NDVI / (1 − NDVI)] cover factor (Van der Knijff et al., 2000) P = 1 no support practice assumed

Soil loss is grouped into five risk classes: very low (≤ 5), low (5–10), moderate (10–20), high (20–40) and very high (> 40 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹). Both districts are mostly flat floodplain, so RUSLE risk is low almost everywhere. River bank erosion is the main hazard, and RUSLE does not describe it.

8

AI prediction of bank erosion

Random Forest (300 trees) and XGBoost (400 trees, depth 6) models learn which land pixels near the river were lost between the two windows. They use 14 predictors measured in the baseline window, such as distance to the channel, river water within 300 m, 1 km and 3 km, height above low ground, slope, NDVI, BSI, MNDWI, water frequency, soil K, the 1984–2021 water history and past bank loss within 1 km. Up to 150,000 land pixels within 3 km of the river are sampled. The trained models are then applied to current conditions to predict where loss is likely next. The final map is the mean of the two models.

Accuracy is measured by spatial block cross-validation: the river is cut into 6 blocks, and each block is predicted by a model that never saw it. This avoids the over-optimistic scores of random splits. The models are compared with two simple baselines: RUSLE soil loss, and distance to the river alone. A model that is no better than chance adds no sites to the priority list.

model skill = (cross-validated ROC-AUC − 0.5) / 0.4, limited to 0–1 susceptibility classes: very low < 0.05 ≤ low < 0.15 ≤ moderate < 0.30 ≤ high < 0.50 ≤ very high
9

Confidence of each result

Every water or land label rests on several dates. With k water dates out of n, a Beta(k + 1, n − k + 1) distribution describes the true water frequency. The confidence of a label is the probability that the frequency lies on the same side of 0.5 as the label. A change needs both labels to be right, so the two confidences are multiplied.

confidence(water) = 1 − I0.5(k + 1, n − k + 1), confidence(land) = I0.5(k + 1, n − k + 1) I = regularised incomplete beta function change confidence = confidencebaseline × confidencecurrent site label: High ≥ 0.90, Medium ≥ 0.75, otherwise Low. For AI sites, the agreement between the two models is used.
10

People and assets at risk, and site priority

Exposure is counted within 500 m of each site. The sources are WorldPop 2020 population, ESA WorldCover 2021 built-up and cropland, and OpenStreetMap roads, embankments and facilities (schools, health centres and similar). Each count is scaled to 0–1 against a saturation value and combined with weights. Hazard comes from the class of the site, its area and its confidence.

12

Accuracy assessment

A stratified random sample of 330 points is drawn from the change map, with more points in rare classes. For each point an analyst looks at the before and after image chips on the labelling page and records the true class. User's and producer's accuracy, overall accuracy and the corrected area of each class, all with 95% confidence intervals, are then estimated by the method of Olofsson et al. (2014) and Stehman (2014). These are good-practice estimators for maps from stratified samples.

area estimate Âj = Atotal × Σh Wh × (nhj / nh) Wh = mapped share of stratum h; nhj = sample points of stratum h whose true class is j
13

Field verification

Each run writes a KoboToolbox/ODK form and a list of priority sites. Field staff pick a site ID, record erosion type, severity, bank height and photographs. Records are matched to sites by ID, or else by the nearest record within 300 m. Each site is then marked confirmed, not confirmed or new. The confirmation rate and the false alarms are shown on each dashboard.

14

Real-time sensor stations (pilot)

Four proposed stations per district sit at the highest-priority bank sites. Each station has a river level sensor, a rain gauge, soil moisture probes at 30, 60 and 100 cm, and tilt nodes 5, 10, 20 and 40 m from the bank edge. Readings are simulated until hardware is installed; the panel can read ThingSpeak channels instead. The rules are checked every minute.

Limitations

The change maps rely on 20 m pixels, so changes smaller than about 0.4 ha are not detected. The char/bank split uses the JRC record, which ends in 2021. On an island district such as Majuli, most of the bank was water at some time since 1984, so much real loss of island land is counted as char loss. RUSLE uses the cell size as slope length, which is a simple approximation for flat floodplains. Population near a site is an upper bound, because WorldPop cells are 100 m. All results should be confirmed in the field before works are planned.
