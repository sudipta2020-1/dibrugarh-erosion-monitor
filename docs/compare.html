How the 10 m baseline method works

  1. 10 m images. The same Sentinel-2 scenes and the same two monitoring windows as the standard run are read at 10 m instead of 20 m.
  2. Sharpened SWIR band. The water index MNDWI needs the short-wave infrared band, which Sentinel-2 records only at 20 m. It is sharpened to 10 m with the 10 m near-infrared band: SWIR10 = SWIR20 × NIR10 / NIR20 (Du et al., 2016).
  3. Water frequency. As in the standard run, every clear date is classified with an edge-based Otsu threshold, and a pixel is water if it was water on at least half of its clear dates.
  4. Smaller minimum patch. Land-to-water change is kept down to 5 pixels of 10 m, which is 0.05 ha. The standard run keeps 10 pixels of 20 m, which is 0.4 ha.
  5. Sub-pixel bank lines. The bank line is traced as the 0.5 contour of the smoothed water-frequency surface with marching squares and linear interpolation, so it can fall inside a pixel (Bishop-Taylor et al., 2019). Bank retreat is measured between the 2020 and current bank lines instead of counting whole pixels.
  6. Comparison. The standard 20 m change map of the latest run is placed on the 10 m grid. Agreement, patch sizes and areas are compared for the river corridor: land within 1 km of the river channels.

Why it should be more accurate. A 10 m pixel holds a quarter of the ground of a 20 m pixel, so mixed land and water pixels along the bank are smaller and fewer. Sub-pixel waterlines have been reported to be several times more accurate than whole-pixel lines. In tests with 30 m Landsat images, errors were about 1.5 to 3.3 m with well-chosen thresholds (Bishop-Taylor et al., 2019).

Limits. Sharpening can add small artefacts at sharp edges. Haze, shadow and wet sand still cause errors, and losses smaller than about 0.05 ha remain below detection. This page compares the two methods with each other. Which one is closer to the truth on the ground must be decided by the accuracy assessment points and field checks.
